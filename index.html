01 / THE BASICS

A spin is one draw from a set of possible outcomes.

At its simplest, a slot has symbols, rules for what pays, and a probability for each possible result. The reel animation is just a way to show you the result. The worked example below derives the odds for the simulator one step at a time.

THE KEY IDEA

Each spin is a new random event. The machine does not need to “remember” what just happened.

02 / THE BIG NUMBER

RTP is an average over time.

Return to player (RTP) is the expected amount returned divided by the amount wagered. In a hypothetical 96% RTP game, the expected return is 96 cents per dollar wagered over many plays; the practice game below works out to 93.2%.

RTP = Σ (outcome probability × gross payout multiplier)
Expected gross return per unit wager
THE IMPORTANT CAVEAT

“Over time” describes a mathematical average across huge numbers of plays. It does not mean you personally get $96 back for every $100 you put in.

03 / THE RIDE

Same average. Different ride.

Variance describes how widely outcomes spread around the average. A low-volatility game tends to pay smaller amounts more often. A high-volatility game can have long dry spells and rare large wins. In the full calculation below, variance comes from the probability-weighted squared distance of each payout from its mean.

REMEMBER

A higher top prize doesn’t mean a better average. The size and probability of every payout both matter.

THE WORKED EXAMPLE SAME RULES AS THE SIMULATOR

From symbol odds to the house edge.

We can calculate every metric exactly because this teaching game has three independent reels, known symbol probabilities, and a complete paytable. Let M be the gross payout in bet units: a 1.25× result returns 1.25 times the stake (including the stake), while a loss returns 0.

1
MODEL THE SPIN

Build the outcome space.

For one reel, the probabilities are pC = 0.25, pL = 0.20, pB = 0.15, pD = 0.10 and pØ = 0.30. They sum to 1. With three independently drawn reels, there are 5³ = 125 ordered symbol triples.

For an ordered triple (a, b, c):P(a, b, c) = pa × pb × pcExample: P(🍒, 🍋, 💎) = 0.25 × 0.20 × 0.10 = 0.005 (0.5%).

“Ordered” matters: 🍒🍋💎 and 💎🍋🍒 are different reel results. Some have the same payout, so their probabilities can be added when grouping outcomes.

2
COUNT A PAYING EVENT

Derive the matching-pair probability.

For a chosen non-blank symbol with probability p, exactly two of three reels show it in C(3, 2) = 3 possible positions. The remaining reel must not show that symbol, which has probability 1 − p.

Exactly two of symbol i:Pi,pair = C(3, 2) pi²(1 − pi) = 3pi²(1 − pi)Cherry: 3 × 0.25² × 0.75 = 0.140625. This excludes three cherries.

3
AVERAGE THE PAYOUTS

Calculate expected return and RTP.

For three matching symbols i, independence gives probability pi³. Multiply each mutually exclusive event’s probability by its gross payout, then sum. This weighted average is the expected gross return per unit bet.

4
NET RESULT & HIT RATE

Return is not the same as profit.

Because gross payout includes the original stake, convert it to net profit by subtracting one bet unit. A pair returns 1.25× but makes only +0.25× net; a losing spin is −1×.

House edge and expected net:Net per spin Y = M − 1; E[Y] = 0.932 − 1 = −0.068 betsHouse edge = 1 − RTP = 1 − 0.932 = 6.8%At a fixed $1 bet, expected net is −$0.068 per spin. Over N spins it is −$0.068N in expectation—not a guaranteed loss of that amount.

Hit rate counts spins with any positive payout, whether or not the payout exceeds the stake. The four triples and exact-pair event are disjoint:

Probability of any payout:Hit rate = 0.015625 + 0.008 + 0.003375 + 0.001 + 0.321 = 0.349 = 34.9%Probability of no payout = 1 − 0.349 = 65.1%. A 1.25× pair is a hit but only +0.25× net.

For an event with probability p, “1 in 1/p” is its average frequency over many trials, not a schedule. A 60× diamond triple has probability 0.10³ = 0.001, or 1 in 1,000 per spin on average; the next spin is still not “due.”

5
SPREAD OF RESULTS

Calculate variance and standard deviation.

The mean alone hides how widely payouts swing. Variance is the probability-weighted squared distance from the mean; squaring keeps both directions positive and gives extra weight to distant outcomes. The net and gross-return variables have the same variance because subtracting a constant does not change spread.

Second moment, variance, and standard deviation:E[M²] = 0.015625(14²) + 0.008(18²) + 0.003375(32²) + 0.001(60²) + 0.321(1.25²) = 13.2120625Var(M) = E[M²] − E[M]² = 13.2120625 − 0.932² = 12.3434385 bet²SD(M) = √Var(M) ≈ 3.5133 bets per spinWith a $b bet: expected net = −0.068b dollars; standard deviation = 3.5133b dollars. The rare large prizes make this distribution highly right-skewed.

Variance is measured in squared units; standard deviation returns to the original unit and is easier to interpret. A large standard deviation does not mean outcomes are uniformly spread: this game loses on 65.1% of spins and has a 60× tail.

6
MANY SPINS

Why observed RTP moves—and tends to settle.

Let Mk be the gross return multiplier on spin k. For equal bets, session RTP is the sample mean. Independent spins add expected values and variances; they do not erase short-run randomness.

Session estimates for N equal-bet spins:Observed RTP = (M₁ + M₂ + ··· + MN) / NE[observed RTP] = 0.932; SD(observed RTP) = 3.5133 / √NExpressed as percentage points, the standard deviation is 351.33 / √N pp. The standard error shrinks like 1/√N, not 1/N.
1,000 spins1-SD reference: 82.1%–104.3%

At this sample size, the standard deviation of observed RTP is about 11.1 percentage points around the 93.2% expectation.

This is a spread-of-sample-means calculation, not a confidence interval or a prediction. A normal approximation can be poor for modest samples because payouts are discrete and strongly skewed. The law of large numbers says the sample average converges toward the expectation as N grows; it does not say when any particular session will get close.

7
EXPERT EXTENSION

Generalize to reels, lines, and features.

The same method applies to more complex games: define all possible outcomes, their probabilities, the wager basis, and the complete payout rules—including feature awards.

Independent reels:P(s₁, …, sr) = ∏j=1r pj(sj)If stops on reel j are equally likely, pj(s) = (number of stops showing s) / (total stops). Unequal virtual-stop weights give unequal symbol probabilities.
Several paylines and overlapping wins:Total payout T = Σj Tj; E[T] = Σj E[Tj]Line outcomes may be dependent, but linearity of expectation still lets you add each line’s expected payout. For variance, dependence matters: Var(T) = Σ Var(Tj) + 2Σi<j Cov(Ti, Tj).
RTP denominator and extra awards:RTP = E[total return, including features] / E[total amount wagered]For a feature that triggers with probability q and awards an additional random amount F, its expected contribution per base spin is q × E[F | feature triggers]. A progressive jackpot contributes its trigger probability times its current award; if that award changes, the expected return changes too. Always use the product’s stated wager and feature rules.

04 / UNDER THE HOOD

Random numbers. Not “hot” reels.

Digital slots typically use a random number generator (RNG). When a play is initiated, the game maps an RNG result to a symbol or outcome using its programmed rules and probability model.

Real-world games are subject to jurisdiction-specific technical and regulatory requirements. This teaching simulator uses your browser’s random number generator and a deliberately small, inspectable model—not a certified casino-game implementation.

NO SECRET TIMING

In a properly designed independent-spin model, waiting longer or changing your timing does not make a particular result “due.”

Feels true. Is it math?

THE MYTH

“It hasn’t paid in ages. It’s due.”

↓
THE MATH

Independent spins don’t keep a balance sheet. A past loss doesn’t increase the probability of a win on the next spin.

THE MYTH

“A near miss means I’m getting closer.”

↓
THE MATH

A near miss is still a loss. Unless the game rules say otherwise, it doesn’t make a future win more likely.

THE MYTH

“96% RTP means I’ll get 96% back.”

↓
THE MATH

That’s a long-run expectation, not a guarantee. A short session can land far above or below that average.

BEYOND THE BASICS YOUR NEXT LAYER

Know what the average leaves out.

σ

Expected value vs. experience

Expected value is the probability-weighted average return. It helps compare models—but doesn’t tell you what one session will look like.

∑

Every outcome counts

To calculate RTP, multiply each possible payout by its probability, then add them all. Rare outcomes can contribute a surprisingly large share.

↗

Progressives change the model

When a jackpot grows, its payout changes. Progressive games can have a changing expected value; always account for jackpot probability and applicable rules.

◎

Hit rate isn’t profitability

A game can pay often but return less than wagered on average. “How often it pays” and “how much it returns” are different measurements.

QUICK REFERENCE THE GLOSSARY

Keep these in your pocket.

RTP

Return to player: expected gross return divided by the wager. For fixed stakes, RTP = E[M], where M is gross payout in bet units.

House edge

The theoretical share of wagers not returned: 1 − RTP. Expected net per unit bet is RTP − 1.

Hit frequency

The probability or observed proportion of spins with any payout. A hit may still return less than the stake.

Expected value

Probability-weighted average: E[X] = Σ pᵢxᵢ. It is an average over repetitions, not a promise for one spin.

Variance

Spread around the mean: Var(X) = E[X²] − E[X]². Standard deviation is √Var(X).

Standard error

Standard deviation of a sample mean. For N independent, equally distributed returns, SE = σ/√N.

Odds vs. probability

For event probability p, 1/p is its average frequency across many independent trials, not a countdown.

RNG

Random number generator: software or hardware producing values used by the game’s outcome-mapping rules.

Paytable

The rules showing which outcomes pay and how much each one returns, including whether awards include the stake.

Independent events

Events where one result does not change the probabilities of the next result.

Linearity of expectation

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y], even when X and Y are dependent. Variances need covariance terms.

Virtual stops

Weighted outcome positions. A symbol’s probability is its eligible stop count divided by total stops only when stops are equally likely.
